Every year, the tequila industry in Jalisco, Mexico, leaves behind about nine hundred thousand tonnes of agave bagasse: the fibre that remains after the sugars are extracted. Most of it is burned, or dumped and left to rot in open piles.

For distilleries it is a cost: between two hundred and five hundred pesos per tonne just to have it hauled away in compliance with regulations, as the process coordinator I interviewed confirmed. For small agave growers it is an environmental problem with no good option, and the residue often ends up burned in the field. Either way, carbon dioxide, particulates and methane end up in rural communities.

My name is Roberto Navarro. I am a biotechnology engineering student in Zapopan, and I have grown mushrooms since I was ten. Fungi Mati turns that bagasse into gourmet mushrooms in four to six weeks.

Here is how it works. A local supplier dries the bagasse, grinds it and compacts it into pellets, which standardize moisture and particle size and store for months. I hydrate and pasteurize them, add twenty percent soybean hull, and inoculate them with oyster mushroom mycelium. The fungus does what it does in nature: its enzymes break down lignin and cellulose, and within four to six weeks the fibre becomes edible mushrooms. What is left goes back to the soil as compost that actually degrades faster than untreated fibre.

That speed is the point. In the time compost takes to half-break down, the same fibre has already become a food that sells by the kilogram. And pelletized bagasse costs about half of a conventional substrate, which means a small grower can prepare it the same way every time.

In 2026, with a grant from Tec de Monterrey, I rehabilitated an abandoned space at the high school of my own university into a permanent outdoor mushroom farm. I taught a cultivation course to fifty students there, harvested twice, and every harvest went back to that community: eighty-five people benefited in total, either by cultivating or by eating the produce. Fungi Mati, as a project, already sells grow kits and courses, some of them to foreigners living in the Guadalajara metropolitan area.

Half of those fifty students learned with an AI tutor I built on ChatGPT with my own cultivation materials, delivered through Google Slides; the other half learned the traditional way. The next version will be a dedicated chatbot built on Claude, so that kit buyers and community growers can get guidance in any language, at any hour.

I also run the fungal strain collection at the Carrillo Biorefinery Lab of Tec de Monterrey, under Dr. Danay Carrillo Nieves. There, an approved research project will test whether crossing two oyster mushroom strains can yield one better adapted to bagasse. It is exploratory, but if it works, the method travels to coffee pulp, corn stover, sugarcane bagasse, any residue you can imagine.

With a five thousand dollar award from the Equitech Futures and Rise Innovation Fund Challenge, I am now building a production farm at home: a thirty-nine litre pressure canner, a laminar flow hood, and a fruiting room with negative pressure, filtered air and ultrasonic humidity. The equipment arrived in September and October, the fruiting room goes up this week, and the next step is my first large purchase of bagasse pellets: one full tonne, after the smaller batches I bought for trials.

In year one, the goal is one tonne of bagasse diverted from burning or improper disposal, and at least six hundred kilograms of gourmet mushrooms sold to restaurants across the Guadalajara metropolitan area through a distributor I already have, grown a few kilometres away instead of trucked for days from other states.
