## Fuentes (abiertas el 5 nov 2026)

1. Poulsen, M. (2015). Towards an integrated understanding of the consequences of fungus domestication on the fungus-growing termite gut microbiota. *Environmental Microbiology* 17: 2562–2572. doi:10.1111/1462-2920.12765 (PDF: https://anthropocene.au.dk/uploads/media/Poulsen2015.pdf)
   > "Approximately 30 million years ago (MYA), the subfamily of higher termites Macrotermitinae domesticated a fungus, Termitomyces, as the main plant decomposer and food source for the termite host"
   > "Older workers collect plant substrate, which is brought back to the nest and passed to younger workers, who eat the crude substrate along with asexual Termitomyces conidiospores produced in the mature fungus comb. This mix is deposited as the primary comb, in which Termitomyces develop dense hyphal networks and produce the next cohorts of nodules."
   > "Within the comb, Termitomyces break down most plant components, such as cellulose, cellobiose and hemicellulose, and as the comb matures new nodules are produced"
   > "as the comb matures and the plant substrate is depleted, older termites consume the old comb in a second and final gut passage"
   > "the termites relying on the fungus for the breakdown of plant material and as a nutrient source"
   > "In most species, a queen and a king – the royal pair – will establish their new nest without the fungus present (Fig. 1A), and the first foragers will collect Termitomyces basidiospores"
   > "Vertical fungus transmission has evolved at least twice: in Macrotermes bellicosus, where the king carries a fungal inoculum in his foregut during colony founding, and in the genus Microtermes, where the queen brings the fungal inoculum"
   > "the fungus is in most termite species transmitted between host generations through the production of spores from fruiting bodies"
   > "include more than 330 described termite species in 12 extant genera … All genera except Hypotermes occur in Africa, while four have colonized Asia and one Madagascar"
   > "This took place in Sub-Saharan Africa"
2. Aanen, D. K. et al. (2002). The evolution of fungus-growing termites and their mutualistic fungal symbionts. *PNAS* 99(23): 14887–14892. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC137514/ (Universidad de Copenhague y otros; muestras de Camerún, Senegal, Gabón, Sumatra, Kalimantan y Sri Lanka)
   > the fungus "helps the termites to degrade the plant-derived material (e.g., wood, dry grass, and leaf litter)"
   > the comb is "maintained by the termites through continuous addition of predigested plant substrate"
   > "fungus growing has a single origin within the termites" · "the symbiosis has a single African origin"
   > "horizontal transmission is the ancestral transmission mode" · vertical transmission is "a derived trait with two independent origins"
   > "Most of the diversity occurs in Africa, where 10 of the 11 genera are found"
3. Roberts, E. M., Todd, C. N., Aanen, D. K., Nobre, T. et al. (2016). Oligocene Termite Nests with In Situ Fungus Gardens from the Rukwa Rift Basin, Tanzania, Support a Paleogene African Origin for Insect Agriculture. *PLoS ONE* 11(6): e0156847. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4917219/
   > fossil fungus gardens "preserved within 25 Ma termite nests from southwestern Tanzania"; age refined to "between ~24.8–24.5 Ma"
   > molecular estimates for termite agriculture recalibrated "to around 31 Ma" (31.41 Ma, IC 95 % 25.82–39.53 Ma; los autores advierten que son un marco de tiempo, no edades absolutas)
   > pellets "(primary faeces)" contain plant fragments and *Termitomyces* spores, which "germinate and colonize the plant material, thus forming fungal gardens"
4. Härkönen, M., Niemelä, T., Mbindo, K., Kotiranta, H. y Piearce, G. (2015). *Zambian mushrooms and mycology*. Norrlinia 29 (Museo Finlandés de Historia Natural). https://www.luomus.fi/sites/default/files/files/zambianmushrooms.pdf
   > *Termitomyces titanicus*: "Cap 40–80 cm in diam., at best 1 m across"
   > "Habitat Arises early in the rainy season in miombo woodlands from old, grass- and shrub-covered termite mounds"
   > "It is well known and eagerly hunted in Zambia, and one fruit body will suffice for a good meal to a large family."
   > "Muddy parts of the stipe are usually peeled off from specimens offered for sale."
   > Fig. 109: "During the rainy season mushrooms are commonly sold along roadsides." (Lusaka–Kabwe main road, Feb 2013)
   > Morfología: sombrero blanquecino "with a smoky brown centre", pie "thick but relatively short … with a hanging ring", pseudorriza "up to 1 m long".

"Granja de hongos" = metáfora del cultivo (las fuentes hablan de "fungus farming", "domesticated", "insect agriculture").

## Datos que NO se usaron
- **"El hongo comestible más grande del mundo"** (Guinness, citado en Härkönen 2015; Oxford Companion to Food): es un récord de libro de récords, no una medición publicada comparativa → no se dice "el más grande"; solo el tamaño del sombrero.
- **"Aire acondicionado" del montículo:** modelo clásico corregido (Turner y Soar; King et al. 2015 PNAS) → no se menciona.
- **Que el hongo descompone la lignina ("madera")**: Poulsen (2015) dice que su papel en la lignina "has been debated and may differ between termite genera" → la voz dice solo "deshace la planta".
- **Las ~40 especies de *Termitomyces* y las 330 especies de termitas**: verificadas (Poulsen 2015) pero se quitaron por tiempo.
- **Aanen 2002 (origen único)**: verificado, pero el estudio elegido para 35–50 s es el de los fósiles de Tanzania (Roberts 2016), con año y lugar más visuales.
