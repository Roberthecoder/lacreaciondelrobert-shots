## Fuentes (abiertas en el texto completo el 29 oct 2026)

1. Hector, R. F. y Laniado-Laborín, R. (2005). Coccidioidomycosis—A Fungal Disease of the Americas. *PLoS Medicine* 2(1): e2. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC545195/
   > "As the soil dries or nutrients become limiting, the fungus reproduces asexually by disarticulating the hyphae into small, environmentally-resistant arthroconidia (reproductive spores)"
   > "These are easily aerosolized when the soil is disturbed by wind or human activities."
   > "Upon inhalation, the fungus converts to a unique life cycle of alternating spherules and progeny endospores, which comprises the parasitic phase of this dimorphic fungus"
   > "spherules are round cells of 30–100 µM or more that reproduce the progeny endospores"
   > "Smith and colleagues found that about 60% of exposures to the fungus result in asymptomatic infection" (dato clásico de los estudios de Smith, 1940s)
   > "it is the inhalation of the dust-borne arthroconidia that leads to infection by this pathogenic fungus in both humans and domestic or wild mammals"
2. Hernandez, H., Erives, V. H. y Martinez, L. R. (2019). Coccidioidomycosis: Epidemiology, Fungal Pathogenesis, and Therapeutic Development. *Current Tropical Medicine Reports* 6(3): 132–144. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8340492/
   > "single-cell barrel-shaped arthroconidia (spores) that are easily aerosolized by any form of soil disturbance"
   > "Once inhaled into the warm lungs of a host, arthroconidia initiate the thermal transition to spherules"
   > mature spherules reach "diameters upwards of 100 μm" and contain "100–300 single-celled endospores"
   > "In 60–65% of individuals, acquisition of Coccidioides spp. leads to asymptomatic sub-clinical infection."
   > "much of the Mexican research focus has been on northern states with deserts … such as the states of Baja California, Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, and Nuevo Leon"; Coccidioides was "later isolated from soil in the northern state of Sonora"
3. CDC Public Health Image Library (PHIL), imagen #12197: artroconidias de *Coccidioides immitis*. https://wwwn.cdc.gov/phil/Details.aspx?pid=12197
   > "The average arthrospore measures 3.0 x 4.5µm, and is barrel-shaped."
4. Schneider, E. et al. (1997). A coccidioidomycosis outbreak following the Northridge, Calif, earthquake. *JAMA* 277(11): 904–908. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9062329/
   > "a coccidioidomycosis outbreak in Ventura County following the January 1994 earthquake, centered in Northridge, Calif"
   > "In Ventura County, between January 24 and March 15, 1994, 203 outbreak-associated coccidioidomycosis cases, including 3 fatalities, were identified"
   > "large dust clouds, generated by landslides following the earthquake and strong aftershocks in the Santa Susana Mountains"
   > "physically being in a dust cloud (odds ratio, 3.0; 95% CI, 1.6-5.4; P<.001) and time spent in a dust cloud (P<.001) significantly increased the risk for being diagnosed with acute coccidioidomycosis"
5. CDC (actualizada el 24 abr 2024). About Valley Fever. https://www.cdc.gov/valley-fever/about/index.html
   > "People can get Valley fever by breathing in the spores from Coccidioides."
   > "Valley fever causes typical lung infection symptoms like cough and fever." (también cansancio/fatigue)
   > "Valley fever does not spread from person to person or between people and animals."
   > "Pets can also become infected."
   > Coccidioides también vive en "parts of Mexico, Central America, and South America".
6. CDC (actualizada el 31 ene 2025). Clinical Overview of Valley Fever. https://www.cdc.gov/valley-fever/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html
